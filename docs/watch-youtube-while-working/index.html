How to watch YouTube while you work

A second monitor is not the only way to keep a video in view. This is the single-screen setup that stays out of your way, including during screen shares.

By Poshith Nandyala Updated November 25, 2026 4 min read
Short answer

Use a small always-on-top YouTube window instead of a second browser tab. Keep it in a corner, fade it to 30–60% opacity, and turn on click-through when you need your mouse. If you share your screen, use a player that can be excluded from capture.

Watching YouTube while you work sounds simple until you try it. A second browser tab disappears behind your editor. A snapped window takes half the screen. Picture-in-picture is always on top, but it is small, hard to control and tied to the browser tab that created it.

The setup that actually works is a small floating window with three controls: pin it above your work, fade it so it does not compete for attention, and let clicks pass through when you need the app behind it. Airframe was built around that workflow.

Why a second monitor is not always the answer

A second display is still the best experience if you have one. But plenty of people work on a single laptop screen, in a shared office, on a train, or with a dock that only wakes one monitor. A floating window uses the screen you already have and does not require a hardware change.

It also fits a different attention pattern. A second monitor encourages a full second window. A small floating video is deliberately peripheral: visible when you glance at it, out of the way when you are reading or typing.

The screen-share problem

If you share your entire screen in Teams, Zoom or Meet, everything on it is fair game: the video, the recommendations sidebar, the tab you meant to close. Moving the window to a second monitor helps only if you share a single display. Sharing the whole desktop can include every monitor.

Airframe's screen-share shield asks Windows to exclude the window from capture. It is on by default and toggles with AltS. In most standard capture paths the video is simply absent or appears black to viewers while you still see it normally. The screen-share guide covers what the shield can and cannot hide.

Two practical rules:

  • Test before the meeting. Start a solo meeting or a local recording, share your screen, and confirm what viewers see.
  • Pause during active discussion. Even a hidden video can distract you, and some capture tools behave differently. AltH hides it instantly.

A five-minute setup

  1. Install Airframe. Use the installer or the portable ZIP from the download section. No admin rights are required.
  2. Open YouTube and pick something long. A playlist, a livestream or a music mix works better than a queue of short videos you will keep checking.
  3. Resize and park it. Drag the top strip to move the window. Put it in a corner that does not cover your editor's minimap, terminal or chat.
  4. Set the opacity. AltShift↓ fades it. Somewhere around 30–60% is a good starting point for background video; 0% makes it invisible but keeps the audio.
  5. Turn on click-through. Bring your editor or browser to the front, then press AltL. Clicks and scrolling now reach the app behind the video. Press it again to take the video window back.

Make it part of the workday

The difference between background video that helps and video that derails you is usually the content and the size, not willpower. A few patterns that work:

  • Music and ambient video: keep the window small and dim. Treat it as audio with a visual hint.
  • Talks and tutorials: keep the window larger while you follow along, then shrink it when you switch to doing the work.
  • Livestreams: park them in a corner and use AltH during calls or deep-work blocks.
  • Focus sessions: hide the video entirely and leave the audio running. The shortcut is faster than finding the tab.

Opacity is the underrated control. At full brightness a video competes with your document for attention. At 40% it becomes part of the desktop, like a wallpaper that happens to move.

When not to use it

A floating video is a bad idea during interviews, exams, client demos, recorded presentations and any meeting where you are the person sharing. It is also a poor fit on battery saver or while rendering video, when the GPU is already busy.

If you need to share your screen and keep the video running, use the shield and test first. If you need to concentrate, close it. The tool is supposed to reduce friction, not become another tab to manage.

The bottom line

You do not need a second monitor to watch YouTube while you work. You need a window that stays on top, takes up only the space you give it, fades into the background and gets out of the way of your mouse. Airframe does those four things without a browser attached.

Frequently asked questions

Can I watch YouTube while working without it showing in Teams or Zoom?

Airframe uses Windows display affinity to exclude its window from most screen captures. Teams, Zoom, Meet and OBS commonly respect it. Test with a recording or a solo meeting before relying on it, because capture tools and company policies vary.

Does a floating YouTube window use a lot of CPU?

Video playback uses some CPU and GPU, but Chromium hardware-decodes most YouTube streams. A small window at 720p is light on a modern laptop. 4K, multiple players and heavy browser tabs are the bigger drains.

Can I listen to YouTube without the video showing?

Airframe does not have a separate audio-only mode, but you can fade the window to 0% opacity or hide it with Alt+H. Playback continues, and Alt+Shift+Up brings the video back.

Does Airframe work with YouTube playlists?

Yes. Airframe loads the normal YouTube site, so playlists, subscriptions, search and your sign-in all work as usual.

Is watching YouTube at work allowed?

That depends on your employer and your contract. Airframe is a playback tool, not a way around company policy. Check your workplace rules before using it during work hours or on a managed device.
